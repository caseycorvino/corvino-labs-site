Connections

If it can't reach one tool, it can't finish the job.

Real work crosses six systems. Miss one and the whole chain stops and waits for a human. So we connect all of them — including the ones nobody else will touch.

The obvious ones

Slack, Gmail, Google Drive, Notion, HubSpot. Ready-made connectors, installed and working the same week.

The ones with an API but no connector

Your billing platform, your scheduling tool, that vertical software your industry runs on. We build the connector against their API.

The ones you built yourself

Internal dashboards and proprietary tools that will never have an off-the-shelf integration. We wrap them so your AI can use them like anything else.

The ones with no API at all

Some vendors just don't offer one. We drive the browser directly — signed in, clicking through the same screens your team does — and turn that into a tool your AI can call.

Hosted wherever it needs to be

On your infrastructure if your security team requires it, on ours if you'd rather not think about it.

Shared, not per-person

Connections are set up once and handed to the whole team, already authorized. Nobody re-does the setup on their own laptop.

Skills

What it can actually do.

When we shadow your team, we find it: somebody has spent a hundred hours teaching AI to do their job well. Everybody else is starting from a blank box.

We take those workflows out of one person's head and turn them into skills the whole company can run — so a new hire on day one has the same playbook as your ten-year veteran.

  • Reporting — the monthly numbers pulled, checked, and written up the way you write them up
  • Drafting — proposals, follow-ups, and briefs in your voice, from your real data
  • Billing and reconciliation — the tedious cross-system checks, run every time
  • Research and search — across your own systems, not just the public web
  • Escalation — knowing what to route to a human, and to which one

Rules

What it's never allowed to get wrong.

Teams don't hand AI the important work because nobody can say what it's allowed to touch. Rules are what make delegation safe enough to be worth doing.

Your managers set them once, and they apply to everyone — enforced as restrictions, not written down in a doc nobody reads.

  • Brand and voice — how it's allowed to sound, and what it can never claim
  • Spend and approvals — what needs a human signature before it happens
  • Data boundaries — which systems it can read, which it can write to, and who it may contact
  • No-guessing rules — where it must say "I don't know" instead of filling in a plausible answer
  • Per-role permissions — the sales team's limits aren't the finance team's limits

Connections, skills, and rules aren't three features sitting side by side. They stack, on top of the AI tool you already bought. Knowing which layer a problem lives in is most of what turns "the AI got it wrong" into something a person can go and fix.

Layer 1 · The AI layer

The part that thinks, and the part we deliberately don't own.

The model, plus the harness wrapped around it. Your team already bought this. We build against it, route work across it, and make sure nothing above it is welded to it.

The harness is the runtime, not the chat box

It runs the tool-calling loop: the model asks for a tool, the harness executes it, feeds the result back, and goes again until the job is done or a limit stops it. It also holds file access, session state, and the context window. The chat window is the least interesting thing it does.

Context is a budget, not a container

A long job overflows the window and the model quietly loses the beginning. So long-running work gets checkpointed and compressed into a fresh session rather than run until it forgets — the real reason a two-hour task and a two-minute task need different plumbing.

Routing, by what the work costs to get wrong

Frontier models run roughly an order of magnitude more expensive than small ones, and most steps in a real workflow don't need one. We route per step, not per tool — which is what keeps the bill flat as usage grows.

One step of a billing workflow Routed to Why
Sorting inbound email Small, fast model Hundreds a day, a one-word answer, no judgment involved. Paying frontier prices here is the most common way an AI bill gets silly.
Pulling line items out of a PDF invoice Small, fast model Structured extraction. Accuracy comes from the schema you validate against, not from the model's reasoning.
Reconciling those line items against the ledger Mid-tier model It has to notice what doesn't match, not just read what's there. Cheap models miss the absence of something.
Explaining the discrepancy to the client Frontier model Judgment and voice. Expensive per call, but it's one call at the end of a hundred cheap ones.
Deciding this one needs a human Frontier model, always The one decision you never want made by the cheap option. Escalation is the safety net — it doesn't get to be the thing you economized on.

What a harness swap actually costs

Everything in layers 2 and 3 is written against a tool interface, not against one vendor's prompt format. Swapping the harness underneath means re-pointing the connections and re-running the skill tests. The workflows, the credentials, the rules and the memory come across unchanged.

Self-hosted models work the same way. If your security team needs the whole thing inside your own network, layer 1 becomes an open-weight model on your hardware and the two layers above it don't notice.

What breaks when this is the only layer you have

This is the state most teams are in: a very capable model with no reach and no boundaries. It will write you a beautiful renewal analysis out of nothing, because it can't see the CRM and nobody ever told it not to guess.

Buying a better model doesn't fix it. The ceiling isn't intelligence — it's that the thing has no hands and no job description.

One tool call. Three checks sit between the model and your billing system, and every one of them is there so that call can’t do something you didn’t intend.

Auth is the hard part, not the API

Connections run on service accounts your IT provisions, not on a person's borrowed login. Tokens refresh on a schedule, rotate without downtime, and revoke in one place. When someone leaves, nothing silently breaks — and nothing silently keeps working.

Rate limits and idempotency

Every vendor throttles differently and every network call eventually fails. Writes carry an idempotency key so a retry can't double-charge a customer or send the same email twice — the failure mode that costs you the most trust for the least reason.

The no-API case, honestly

When a vendor offers nothing, we drive a browser in a real signed-in session and expose it as a tool like any other. It works, and it's the most fragile thing we build — a vendor redesign breaks it. So it's monitored, and it fails loudly rather than returning something wrong.

A skill is a procedure, not a prompt

It's checked into version control with a name, the tools it may call, its steps, the checks that have to pass, and what it does when one doesn't. You can diff it, review it, and roll it back. A clever paragraph in one person's chat history can't be any of those things.

Memory, scoped on purpose

What it learned last month about your invoicing quirks is stored, indexed and searchable — but scoped to the work it belongs to. One shared brain holding everything gets worse at every individual job, and makes "who can see this" impossible to answer.

Hosted where your security team needs it

The same build inside your VPC or on our infrastructure. That decision changes where the credentials sit and who holds the logs. It doesn't change the connections, the skills, or anything your team sees.

This layer is portable by construction, and that is the whole argument for building it properly. Skills and rules outlive harnesses; harnesses have been lasting about eighteen months.

Layer 3 · The human layer

The layer that decides what the other two are allowed to do.

A rule isn't a paragraph in a prompt asking nicely. It's a check sitting in the path of the call. It runs before anything leaves your building, and it can stop the call outright.

Hard blocks

Things that never happen, full stop. A claim the brand can't make. A system this role has no business writing to. The call doesn't go out, and the attempt is logged.

Approval gates

Things that happen after a named person signs off. Spend over a threshold, anything client-facing on a named account, any write to the system of record. The work still gets done — it waits at a desk you chose.

Must-declare

Where it has to say "I don't know" rather than produce a plausible number. This is the rule that earns the trust the other two spend, and the one teams most often forget to write.

One afternoon of the rule log. Every line carries a role, a rule name and a timestamp — which is what makes an AI decision reviewable a month later, instead of a thing that just happened.

Every rule has an owner with a name

Rules are set per role by the manager who owns that function — the finance lead writes the spend limits, the brand lead writes what it may claim. Nobody has to write rules for a job they don't run, and the sales team's limits are not the finance team's limits.

That's also what makes them maintainable. A rule with no owner is a rule nobody updates when the business changes.

Escalation is a designed path, not a shrug

When work stops, it stops at a specific person, with what they need to decide in under a minute: what it was doing, which rule caught it, and what it recommends. Held work that lands in a queue nobody owns is the same as work that failed.

Training belongs to this layer too. Gates only work if the people on the receiving end know what they are and why one fired.

All three layers, one request

"Why did we lose the Harlow renewal?"

A question somebody asks in Slack on a Tuesday. It isn't a hard question. It's just spread across four systems, which is why it usually gets answered with a guess.

Five hops across three layers — and the only two that need a person are the first one and the last one.

  • Pick the skill — a small model recognizes this as a churn post-mortem rather than a general question, and loads the skill your revenue lead wrote. No frontier call yet.
  • Reach four systems, run the checks — the CRM for account history, the billing platform for the invoice record, the support desk for tickets, the shared mailbox for the thread. The support desk has no public connector, so it goes through one we built. Then the skill runs its actual steps: build the timeline, match tickets to invoice events, check whether the QBR happened, confirm who the champion was and whether they are still there.
  • Write the finding — three support tickets about export failures in February, the March invoice disputed and paid late, the champion gone in April, the Q2 QBR never rescheduled after they cancelled it. The answer wasn't in any one system. It was in the order those four things happened.
  • Rule check, then you — the finding names a person who left and a disputed invoice, so it goes nowhere near the account team automatically. It goes to the revenue lead, who decides what the rest of the company hears.

Take away the operational layer

It can only see what somebody pastes into it. The answer comes back as "they churned in Q2" — correct, useless, and delivered with total confidence.

Take away the human layer

The finding is accurate, and it emails the account team a summary naming a departed champion and a disputed invoice twenty minutes before anyone senior has read it.

Take away the AI layer

Somebody spends two hours in four tabs building the timeline by hand. Which is exactly why this analysis has never once been run on an account you didn't lose.

Rollout

Everybody gets it, or it didn't work.

It starts with shadowing and it ends with the whole team. A pilot with three enthusiastic volunteers proves nothing. We install the same connections, skills, and rules for every person who needs them, and train the team on what changed.
