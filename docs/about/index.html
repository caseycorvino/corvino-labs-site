The origin

Lavender, and the ten years of being the person who had to make it work.

Corvino Labs did not start with a thesis about AI. It started with a decade of shipping software under the two constraints every company actually has: not enough people, and customers who notice immediately when something breaks.

Before

Small teams, real customers, no room for theatre

Casey finished New York University in three years and went straight to being the CTO of an early-stage startup automating fashion PR. It set the pattern for everything after: a team too small to specialise, a budget too thin to buy your way out of a problem, and software that had to work on Monday morning regardless.

Sorter

The problem underneath the problem

He led engineering on Sorter alongside Will Allred and William Ballance — applying personality and communication psychology to marketing. The lesson that outlasted the company: the hard part of communication software is never generating the words. It is knowing what good looks like, and being able to say so before the message goes out.

2020

Sorter becomes Lavender

The three of them rebuilt it around a single sharp job: coach a salesperson on the email they are writing, in real time, while they can still change it. Casey co-founded Lavender as CTO, owning product and engineering. The company scored every email against what it had learned from millions of real ones and told the rep exactly what to fix before they hit send.

The bet

Use the models, don’t build them

Casey adopted large language models early — before the tooling was good and well before it was fashionable — and made a deliberate call not to staff a research team to do it. That decision is the single most load-bearing idea we brought with us, and it is why we will never try to sell you a model.

“I don’t have a Ph.D. and don’t know how to build an ML model. Yet, with democratization, I adopted Large Language Models (AI models that specifically generate text) to co-found Lavender.”

Casey Corvino — co-founder & CTO, Lavender

At scale

Tens of thousands of people depending on it

Lavender grew past 20,000 active users across Twilio, Clari, Sendoso, Demandbase, Segment, MasterClass and thousands of smaller teams, and raised $13.2M in funding, including an $11M Series A led by Norwest Venture Partners. Running AI for that many people, every day, on their real work, is a different discipline from demonstrating it once. That difference is most of what we sell.

Now

Corvino Labs

Companies kept asking a version of the same question: we bought the AI, why is nothing different? The answer was always the same three gaps, and we had already spent years closing them for ourselves. So we started a company to close them for other people’s teams.

What it taught us

Four things we learned the expensive way.

Our method is not a framework somebody drew on a whiteboard. Every step of it exists because skipping it cost us something at Lavender first.

01

You do not need a research team

Instead of hiring AI researchers at enormous expense, Lavender hired backend engineers to use the models that already existed. It saved millions and kept the product affordable enough to sell. Your company is in the same position, which is why Foundation works inside the AI tools you already pay for rather than selling you a new vendor.

02

The model was never the hard part

Almost none of the difficulty was intelligence. It was access, plumbing and the twelve unglamorous integrations that had to hold. That is why our second step is connecting every tool you run — including the ones with no API — and why we do not stop at the easy three.

03

Judgment is the product, not the text

Lavender was never a generative AI company; it was a communication intelligence company that had read millions of emails to learn what worked. Anyone can generate a draft. The value is in encoding what your best people know and handing it to everybody else — which is exactly what step three does with your workflows.

04

Real users break what demos never touch

Twenty thousand people using something daily on live data find every edge the sample dataset politely avoided. We do not show you anything that has not run on your real records, and we would rather disappoint you in week one than in month four.

Our mission

Every person on your team, working like your best one.

Inside every company there is already one person getting extraordinary leverage out of AI. They spent a hundred private hours getting there, the results are real, and almost none of it has reached anybody else. That gap is the whole problem, and it widens every month it is left alone.

Our mission is to close it — to make every person at a company as effective as its best one, by installing the same connections, the same encoded expertise and the same rules for everybody, not just for the one who figured it out alone.

We measure ourselves on one thing: whether the work actually moved off your team’s plate. Not seats activated, not prompts written, not enthusiasm in a training session. Work absorbed, by people who were not the early adopter.

What we believe

The opinions we will not be talked out of.

  • Watch the work before you automate it. A week of shadowing costs less than a quarter spent building something impressive that nobody needed. It is the step everyone skips and the reason most of these projects automate the wrong thing.
  • Rules are what make it usable, not what slow it down. Nobody hands real work to something they cannot predict. Managers decide what may be delegated, and those decisions get enforced rather than documented and hoped for.
  • A pilot with three volunteers is not a rollout. If the ten-year veteran and the hire starting next month do not have it, you have not changed how your company works — you have given one person a hobby.
  • Being model-agnostic is a requirement, not a feature. Everything we build for you should survive you switching AI vendors next year, because you probably will.
  • You should be able to fire us. We hand over something your own team can run, change and explain. Plenty of clients keep us on — but nothing we build depends on us being in the room.
  • We will tell you when something is not worth automating. There is always at least one item on the list, and it is usually the one somebody was most excited about.

If any of that sounds like a swipe at how AI usually gets sold into companies, it is. We watched it work the other way for years and we are not interested in rebuilding the version that quietly dies.
