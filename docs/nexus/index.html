When is it time to hire an AI employee?

Outgrowing foundations.

Teams that are early with AI should not start here. But once your whole team has AI wired into everything and is genuinely using it, the next limit shows up fast: one generic assistant holding everyone's context, everyone's tools, and everyone's rules at the same time is worse at every individual job than a focused one would be.

Nexus splits it up the way a company does. Each AI employee has a role, and only the skills, connections, memory, and rules that role needs. Better focus, cheaper to run, and far easier for a manager to reason about.

Everything below is the actual specification: how an employee is defined, where it runs, how work moves, and what stops it.

A session can only use the accounts its own employee holds. Not “can only see” — can only use. It fails closed: an identity that cannot be resolved gets nothing, never everything.

Harness-agnostic

Runs where you want

Your cloud, your server, or ours. Task history and memory live in a database you can back up, read and export without asking us.

The hand-off is one line, on purpose

An employee ends a run by stating a verdict in a fixed form: done, or blocked with the question. A reviewer states pass, fail with what is wrong, or blocked.

That line is the whole contract. The employee never moves its own card; the dispatcher reads the verdict and moves it. An agent that can promote its own work has a polite suggestion of a review gate.

  • No verdict is not a pass. A review that ends without one costs an attempt and the card stays where it is
  • Blocked means stopped. The run ends, the lock releases, and the question goes to the manager — it does not guess to keep the lane moving
  • Three failed attempts and the card blocks itself with the reason, instead of burning budget in a loop
  • A clean hand-off resets the count, so normal rework never trips the circuit breaker

Two review gates

Nothing reaches you until another employee has checked it.

The stage most AI tools are missing sits between “done” and “your problem.” Work goes to a second AI employee for review first, and only reaches a person once it has survived that.

Your attention is the scarcest thing in the system, so it is spent last — on work that has already been checked by someone whose entire job was checking it.

What gate 1 can return

PASS

It holds up

The reviewer re-derived the claims and agrees. The card moves to your lane with the reviewer’s note attached, so what you read is a second opinion, not a first draft.

FAIL

It goes back, with notes

Straight to the top of the queue with what is wrong. The next run gets those notes in its brief, so it picks up where the last attempt stopped instead of starting over. You are not involved.

BLOCKED

It needs a decision

The reviewer hit a question only a person can answer. The run stops, the question goes to you, and your reply re-queues the card with the answer attached.

Rework between two AI employees is the cheapest loop in the system and the one you never see. That is the point: the expensive reviewer is you, so gate 1 exists to waste as little of gate 2 as possible.

Why a second employee, not a second prompt

They check each other’s work.

A reviewing employee does not just re-read the draft. It has its own tools, credentials and context, so it goes back to the source data, runs the numbers itself, and disagrees when they say something different.

This is the practical argument for a fleet: asking a model to double-check itself gets you the same blind spot twice. A different employee with different access catches what the first one never would.

  • Different credentials — the reviewer reads the source system directly, not the draft’s summary of it
  • Different rules — a reviewer’s rules are about verification, not production
  • No editing — a reviewer returns a verdict. It never quietly fixes the work and passes it

Dedicated, not shared

They are real employees. They have their own accounts.

A Nexus employee does not borrow a human’s login. It gets its own email address, its own chat handle and its own seat in your CRM, provisioned, permissioned, and revocable like anyone else on payroll.

Its own identity in every system — so every action it takes is attributable to it, not to whoever’s credentials it borrowed.

This is the part most AI tools skip, and it is the part that makes the difference between a clever assistant and something you can actually put on the org chart.

  • Own accounts — real logins in Gmail, Slack, Salesforce and everywhere else it works, provisioned by your IT the same way a new hire’s are
  • Siloed memory — it remembers its own work and its own context. Nothing leaks between roles, and nothing bloats into one shared brain
  • Its own permissions — scoped to the job. Your finance employee can reach the billing system; your social employee cannot
  • Its own rules — what this specific role may never do, set by the manager it reports to
  • Its own skills — the workflows for its job, not a catalog of everything the company does
  • Its own training — onboarded on your systems, your voice and your process, and retrained as they change

Because every action runs under its own identity, your existing audit logs, access reviews and offboarding process already work. Turning an AI employee off is the same as turning any employee off.

Running lean

Your subscriptions do the work, not metered API pricing.

Metered API access is priced per token, and it adds up fast at real usage. Nexus runs on the flat-rate subscriptions your team already pays for instead, and only reaches for metered API when a subscription genuinely cannot do the job.

Metered APIExisting subscriptions

Auto picks the model. Every run routes through Auto, which sends the job to the cheapest model that can actually do it, and steps up to a frontier model only when the work calls for it.

Context stays lean. A long-running thread gets compressed and handed off with only what the next step needs, instead of dragging the whole history along and paying to re-read it every time.

Subagents take the side work. Research, verification and narrow sub-tasks run in their own isolated calls, so the main job never pays premium-model rates for work a cheaper pass could do.

Every run reports what it cost, per card and per employee.
